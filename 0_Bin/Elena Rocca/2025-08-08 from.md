You are absolutely right when you say that the transition from provisional to established knowledge is blurred in some cases, and the case of establishing causality between a drug and an adverse event is definitely an example. There are many reasons for it, maybe the most prominent is that rare ADRs are not visible in clinical trials. In establishing the knowledge there are also a lot of ethical considerations of ‘inductive risk’ type. Such considerations often push imperfect knowledge to the category of ‘good enough’, when important potential public health risks are at stake.

But before talking about the messy and difficult cases, I wonder how your analogy between knowledge flow and energy flow could help in the very ‘neat’ cases, when the transition to established knowledge is clear. After reading your published example of chemistry, for which your framework works well, I still do not understand how this would work in medicine, and especially what would be the purpose of your model: would it be predictive for similar cases?

If you have the patience to try to explain me, could you show how your thinking would apply to the example of oestrogen replacement therapy's effect on coronary heart disease? The therapy was thought to be protective based on observational studies, until a big clinical trial showed the opposite effect.
