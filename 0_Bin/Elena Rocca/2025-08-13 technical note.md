Technical Note – Thermodynamic Analysis applied to ERT–CHD
The article you sent argues for the need to make explicit the mechanisms leading to the
observed phenomenon. Against the Evidence-Based Medicine (EBM) stance, which tends to
treat evidence of mechanisms and evidence of correlations as exclusive, the article proposes
that these types of evidence should be regarded as complementary for improving result
reliability. This reading may be somewhat simplified given my unfamiliarity with the current state
of the debate. I understand, for example, that disagreements may arise in cases of discordance,
when a mechanism-based hypothesis yields one result while a higher-quality correlation-based
study yields a different, incompatible one.
The general thesis of your paper appears aligned with my article on multimodal discordance. In
that article, the first figure (copied below) could be adapted to medical cases by replacing
“reading” with “clinical sign”. The key point is that results (the outcomes) are not directly about
the element under study (the sample) but through a theoretical framework that situates them
within a broader context—in your terms, the mechanism. This aligns with the well-known
recognition that “data are theory-laden”. In scientific contexts, a result is always interpreted
through a theoretical framework —which includes the causal mechanism producing the result.

Aligned to this, to introduce the thermodynamic framework, it is useful to distinguish between
the robustness of individual results (“nodes”) in scientific networks and the robustness of
patterns—configurations of multiple nodes (and sometimes non-robust elements) that form a
coherent whole. An analysis focused on a single node is referred to as parametric, whereas an
analysis focused on a pattern is called systemic.
In biology, patterns often include mechanisms, such as mechanism M in your paper. EBM can
be interpreted as focusing on the robustness of a single node (a population-based correlation).

While this focus is not necessarily wrong, nodes are always embedded in complex networks,
and ignoring the systemic perspective can make the analysis incomplete.
Depending on the study’s aims, elements of the whole theoretical framework are abstracted until
defining the minimal coherent pattern containing the node. The formal specification of this
pattern corresponds to the “thermodynamic condition” for considering the object of study stable.
I will try to explain how next.
The thermodynamic quantity used is: G = H − TS, such that:
● G determines system stability. Changes producing ΔG < 0 are spontaneous.
● A minimum G for a parameter P means no change in P can move the system (i.e., ΔG >
0 always).
● Stability means G is minimal for all parameters, so no spontaneous transformations
occur.
● Technologically, devices use a shell to cover the system and separate it from the
environment, minimizing changes that would cause ΔG < 0.
On the other hand, in robustness analysis, a robust pattern is stable under environmental
changes—analogous to a stable thermodynamic system (Chalita, 2015, ch. 3). Here,
● H is robustness exchange potential (capacity for robustness exchange with the
environment).
● T measures how node robustness affects the whole pattern.
● S is variability in robustness across nodes.
Multimodal determination of nodes alone tends to increase H and decrease S, thereby raising G.
This is not consistent with the premise of minimizing G to achieve stability. Therefore, a systemic
rather than merely a parametric analysis is needed. A systemic analysis considers not only the
robustness of nodes within a pattern but also specifies which nodes belong to the pattern. This
is equivalent to a thermodynamic interface separating the system from the environment. Surface
Thermodynamics provides formulas to describe systems that are not clearly separated from
their environment, such as a cell in microbiology.
Such a systemic analysis can be applied to the ERT–CHD case by interpreting how the relative
weights of mechanistic evidence and correlations contribute to epistemic stability or instability. In
this light, a thermodynamically “stable” epistemic state would correspond to a situation in which

no plausible change in the evidential environment—whether in correlation studies or
mechanistic understanding—could overturn the conclusion. This mirrors the scientific moment
when a claim is treated as established and further investigation is considered unnecessary
except for refinement or exceptional challenges.
The mathematical details of this connection and the further formal development of interface
terms can be explored in future work. I have also omitted, for now, the derivation showing the
condition a pattern must satisfy to become solid (epistemically stable). This condition is linked to
validation in a context with lower parametrization, such as that found in correlation studies. In
conclusion, while mechanistic understanding is necessary to enable a complete
parametric/systemic analysis of a phenomenon, correlation studies are necessary to bring that
phenomenon to epistemic stability. This reflects a virtuous dynamic between the two types of
studies, reinforcing the view that they must be regarded as complementary, and that
discordance between them should be explicitly and critically problematized.
