An emergence account in philosophy of science holds that some properties or behaviors arise at higher levels of organization that are not straightforwardly reducible to, predictable from, or explainable solely in terms of lower-level constituents and laws, even though they depend on them. Classic examples include thermodynamic properties (like temperature), biological functions, or collective phenomena in complex systems, which emerge from interactions among parts rather than from the parts taken in isolation. Emergence accounts typically stress novelty (the higher-level features are qualitatively new) and autonomy (higher-level explanations have their own explanatory legitimacy), while remaining compatible—at least in weak forms—with physicalism. Stronger versions add claims about irreducibility or even downward causation, whereas weaker versions interpret emergence as reflecting limits of derivation, modeling, or explanatory practices rather than new fundamental entities.

A related issue is Gilbert Ryle’s notion of _category distinctions_ and _category mistakes_. According to Ryle, a category mistake occurs when something belonging to one logical category is presented as if it belonged to another—for example, treating “the university” as an additional object alongside colleges, libraries, and staff, rather than as the organized way in which these elements function together. Category distinctions mark differences in the kinds of things we are talking about (objects, dispositions, processes, institutions, abilities), and philosophical confusion arises when these distinctions are ignored. For Ryle, many traditional metaphysical problems stem not from deep ontological facts but from misclassifying concepts and thereby asking the wrong sort of question.

Emergence can be linked to category distinctions by interpreting some claims of irreducibility as reactions to category mistakes rather than as discoveries of new entities or forces. On this view, emergent properties are not mysterious additions to the ontology but belong to a different explanatory or conceptual category than the micro-level descriptions from which they depend. Treating higher-level patterns, functions, or organizations as if they had to be derivable in the same terms as lower-level physical properties risks a category mistake, analogous to asking where the university is located after listing its buildings. Emergence, especially in its weaker forms, can thus be understood as signaling a legitimate shift in descriptive and explanatory categories rather than a failure of reduction in principle.

Margaret Morrison argues that higher-level phenomena often require autonomous modeling frameworks that capture patterns of organization, stability, or constraint that are not visible at the level of fundamental equations alone. On this view, the autonomy associated with emergence reflects a shift in explanatory category—from micro-dynamical descriptions to structural or organizational descriptions—rather than the postulation of novel ontological ingredients. Read through a Rylean lens, Morrison’s position suggests that insisting on a single, bottom-level mode of description for all phenomena risks a category mistake: it conflates the category of fundamental laws with that of explanatory models tailored to specific kinds of phenomena. Emergence, in this sense, marks the legitimate pluralism of scientific categories and representational practices, not a breach in the ontological unity of science.
