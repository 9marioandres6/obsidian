An emergence account in philosophy of science holds that some properties or behaviors arise at higher levels of organization that are not straightforwardly reducible to, predictable from, or explainable solely in terms of lower-level constituents and laws, even though they depend on them. Classic examples include thermodynamic properties (like temperature), biological functions, or collective phenomena in complex systems, which emerge from interactions among parts rather than from the parts taken in isolation. Emergence accounts typically stress novelty (the higher-level features are qualitatively new) and **autonomy** (higher-level explanations have their own explanatory legitimacy), while remaining compatible—at least in weak forms—with physicalism. Stronger versions add claims about irreducibility or even downward causation, whereas weaker versions interpret emergence as reflecting limits of derivation, modeling, or explanatory practices rather than new fundamental entities.
