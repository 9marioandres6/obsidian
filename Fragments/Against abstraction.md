The stakes of the debate around the concept of abstraction seem especially pressing, since abstraction has good reason to be the favoured mount of philosophy of science in practice in its long crusade against the old and entrenched conception of representation as a mirror image of nature. Attempts to dismantle that conception have occupied philosophy of science for almost a century, at least since Hanson and Kuhn, yet a still-powerful structural realism continues to hold its ground, maintaining in its different versions that some structure must be shared between theory and world—or, in the vocabulary now more customary, between model and target.

When called upon to take the field, the philosopher of science in practice raises a banner that appears difficult to assail: models present us with distorted versions of the world, and their scientific usefulness frequently depends precisely on those distortions. Idealisation, omission, simplification, approximation and deliberate misrepresentation have accordingly supplied some of the strongest weapons against any conception of representation grounded in faithful resemblance.

As a payback, the measure of this supposed distortion is increasingly what the concept of abstraction has been asked to provide. How, then, could anyone question abstraction without being singled out as a suspected heretic against the body of evidence that sociologists of science have painstakingly assembled? The present work, therefore, undertaken a dangerous task: to show that scientific practices themselves can be understood more genuinely once abstraction, long deployed as a cannon against representationalism, is laid aside as technical terminology.
