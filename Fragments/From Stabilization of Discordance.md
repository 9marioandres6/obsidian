Pointing to irreconcilable disagreement across a wide range of scientific areas provides a direct argument for methodological pluralism, whether the plurality concerns methods of empirical determination, models, theories, environmental contexts or institutionally endorsed interpretations. Wave–particle duality already gives this point a canonical form: the same domain can require mutually incompatible descriptive frameworks whose use depends on the investigative arrangement. A comparable plurality appears in the classification of petroleum fractions, which may be organised by carbon-chain length, boiling range or solubility class; methane, butane and octane belong to one classificatory regime, oil, naphtha and natural gas to another, and saturates, aromatics, resins and asphaltenes to a third. A further example emerges from discordance in climate science, where different models may attribute observed temperature changes to distinct combinations of atmospheric, oceanic and anthropogenic factors. These models often remain in tension while nevertheless contributing to explanation and prediction, with their relative usefulness depending on the scale, purpose and evidential constraints of the inquiry.

This de facto pluralism has long coexisted with a recurrent tendency to suppress, reduce or normalise it, a tendency that forms a significant part of what counts as ordinary scientific work. Pluralism in scientific practice can therefore be described as permitted yet undesirable. Faced with this tension, reductive monist programmes respond by suppressing the permitted side of the ambivalence, treating plurality as a temporary obstacle to the unity of science (Oppenheim & Putnam, 1958). Pluralist strategies often respond by softening the undesirable side, treating plurality as epistemically acceptable once local aims, standards and contexts of inquiry are acknowledged (Longino, 2002, Chapter 6).
