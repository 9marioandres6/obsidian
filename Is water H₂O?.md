Questions of the form of this section’s title have been repeatedly raised in recent philosophy of science and philosophy of chemistry, often with the aim of reassessing the relation between scientific kinds, microstructural constitution, and realist commitment (Chang 2012; Earley 2014). While these discussions differ in scope and motivation, they tend to frame the problem as one of identity, reduction, or semantic reference: whether the ordinary and scientific concept of water can be identified with a specific microstructural description, or whether such an identification must be relativised to conceptual, historical, or pragmatic contexts. What is less often examined is whether the very form of the question presupposes a representational schema that is ill-suited to capture how scientific results become stabilised and treated as real within practice.

On a pattern enactment account, water is not first identified by a privileged description and then represented by alternative models or theories (Wimsatt 2007, pp. 60–61; p. 89). Instead, what comes to count as water is progressively enacted through a coordinated set of experimental practices, measurement procedures, classificatory operations, and inferential linkages that stabilise a pattern across heterogeneous modes of determination. Chemical formulae, phase diagrams, thermodynamic regularities, laboratory techniques, and everyday practices of identification all contribute to the enactment of a pattern that constrains what can count as water and how it can be reliably tracked across contexts.

Within this framework, the formula H₂O is not the essence of water, nor merely a conventional label attached to it (Mari & Giordani 2023). It functions as one node within a broader enacted pattern that has achieved a high degree of stabilisation. The realist force of H₂O does not derive from its correspondence to an underlying metaphysical structure taken in abstraction, but from its role in coordinating a wide range of determinations: predicting phase transitions, explaining chemical reactions, supporting measurement standards, guiding experimental manipulation, and enabling reliable intervention. The question of whether water is H₂O should therefore be recast as a question about how the H₂O-description becomes entrenched as a fixed point within the enacted pattern through which water is tracked and coordinated in scientific practice.

This reframing helps clarify why familiar alternative answers to the identity question are unsatisfactory (Scerri 2007). Conventionalist or descriptivist approaches treat the link between water and H₂O as a matter of definition or linguistic decision, thereby underplaying the empirical and practical constraints that make certain identifications durable and others fragile. Reductionist approaches, by contrast, locate the reality of water entirely at the microstructural level, treating higher-level regularities and practices as derivative or merely epistemic. Both strategies miss the fact that stabilisation occurs neither purely at the level of convention nor solely at the level of microphysical description, but through the coordinated enactment of patterns that cut across these levels.

Recent historical and practice-oriented analyses of chemical kinds have shown that the concept of water has been shaped by multiple, partially independent lineages, including compositional analysis, thermodynamic characterisation, and practical techniques of purification and identification (van Brakel 2000). What these studies bring out is not a failure of realism, but the manner in which realist commitment is achieved gradually, as disparate determinations are aligned and rendered mutually supportive. Pattern enactment supplies the explanatory step: it shows how such alignments yield a result that can be treated as real without presupposing a correspondence-based account of representation.
