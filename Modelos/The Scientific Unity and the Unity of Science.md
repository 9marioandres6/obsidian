
# 4. Interaction between the scientific model and the measuring instrument in scientific and technological activity

## 4.7 The world-nature tension expressed in the hypothesis, in the experiment and in technological production

Extremely exceptional are those cases in which the measurement is performed on a system that has not been technologically touched. But the fact that it has been technologically modified is not enough for it to be considered a scientific system. The object that is interesting to science is not only the one that is available to know it, but the one that science produces to therefore come to know it (Hacking 1983, ch. 13; 1992). When science technologically intervenes on an object to observe its properties, the result is what is known as a scientific experiment, which is no longer just a measurement of things that can usually be found in daily life outside the laboratory. Rather reverse the equation: the formal background of those things we usually find today outside the laboratory were once forced into existence in a laboratory experiment for the first time. With this key it is possible to read another corollary of the scientific-technological cycle studied in this section: science naturalizes the world, technology intervenes on the natural object known by science, and finally the world absorbs the technological product whose scientific foundations have been hidden for the user. There is a very interesting point of analysis in the last step, which can be called the “interface effect” of the technological object, according to which a non scientist can use a technically very complex object without knowing the vast majority of the principles of its construction. Technological objects have their usage models well differentiated from their construction models. The interface effect is precisely what allows this differentiation, and it will not be analyzed here further, mainly because its study in terms of philosophy of science is today practically nil.

In section 3.2 it was said, within the framework of the distinction between nature and world, that a new model rises above the everyday world, and this always offers resistance and anchors the former, so that, in order to transcend the community, the proposed model cannot mark an excessive distance with this knowledge. Now, in view of the recent discussion, it is noted that the daily knowledge is in turn in many ways influenced by technical knowledge, either because many scientific concepts and worldviews according to scientific models end up becoming over time in part of the cultural baggage of the society and they are taught, for example, in regular schools, either because technological products, always built at least in part based on scientific models, also become common currency. This allows to speak of a kind of feedback in which science has as a daily anchor for its development, but this at its time is in turn historically being modified in the direction in which science has advanced, so that the successive new scientific models start from an increasingly technical soil, and it is assumed that consequently they will be able to go further in their proposal in comparison to more "precarious" models whose hypotheses emerge from non/less-technical knowledge.

But there is a second consideration to understand why science has an even longer string than that suggested by its direct relationship with the non-technical world to trace the kite of its speculation, and this extension is related to the interface of the technological instrument. The possibility of covering from the user the scientific foundations of a technological object allows the engineer to evade the limit of the user's knowledge for its use. If it is accepted, as suggested in section 3.2, that it is a condition for the acceptance of a model that it is applicable at least potentially, it should be noted that the problem of applicability, and therefore that of the acceptance of a scientific model resides largely in the ability of engineers to design a technological object whose interfaces allows it to convert the technical use of the sum of parts to the daily use of the object as a whole. This, well read, means that when evaluating a scientific model, the community is obliged to consider its applicability as potential—since for the design of technological objects based on a scientific model, the model must already be available—but only when this applicability becomes a matter of fact (this has historically been the case each time) the models are fully accepted (here still following Hacking 1992). And this has a very powerful reason: that for the production of a technological object it is necessary to repeat with high precision what science did in the laboratory for the first time, so the production of technological objects is proof of the accuracy of the scientific model adopted by the engineer that no one can deny.

To the effort of imagination that the scientist or creator of a model must make in semantic terms to convert part of everyday language into technical language and thus contribute to the knowledge of nature it is added, as condition for the full acceptance of the model, the design effort of engineers to ideate an object that conceals its fundamentals to the user. In between these two, a third effort of imagination must still be added, which is certainly in the very limit between the scientific and the technological, which consists in the design of the contrastation experiment by which the model must begin to consider itself accepted. These three efforts of imagination that coordinate with each other according to the stage of confirmation in which the model is, are three aspects of the tension between the world and nature that has been introduced in section 3. It is not superfluous to clarify that, for each of these three stages listed, the world-nature tension manifests itself in the novel aspect each time, not in the other unitary elements that the model already takes from the available natural knowledge, with a markedly lower level of effort and controversy.

The scientific experiment has its center of gravity in a measurement event and in a measurement system, but certainly it is not considered an experiment to that measurement that is carried out without in any way tensioning the object being measured. This is the essential meaning of the term "laboratory": that context that allows controlled modification of the object to be measured. How far can science go in this context modification to make its measurement? Assuming that it is not a situation in which the context is modified for the sole purpose of observing what happens, but that the modification is guided by a hypothesis, then an idea of ​​such distance will be given by the hypothesis. But the most forceful benchmark will be given by the technology available for the time, and this is also the main factor that the hypothesis must consider: it can rise only to a certain extent in speculation over the available contrast capacity, and in the case of such excess it will have to wait for the technology to develop so that it can be contrasted, being unable in the meantime to do more than devise imaginary experiments—which are themselves an institution and their general meaning is none other than that: to be lawful devices to imagine a measurement situation when the technology to carry out such measurement is not available.

Formalizing as isomorphic the relationship between the model and its target, understanding scientific representation as a mathematical function, should not be a direct and caution-free step. The first indication is that the relationship between the target and the model does not need to become a function, since the point of interest is the equivalence relationship between their respective patterns, of which it has been said in section 1.4 above that it is not appropriate to be considered as a representation. Rather, both patterns must be equivalent so that the material correlative systems in which they are embedded can give rise to representation.

Thus, due to the equivalence between both patterns, the corresponding function is the identity function. This reflects the simplicity of the isomorphic relationship, but brings a complication with the following question, which would not be difficult to tackle if the relationship were not symmetrical: which of the patterns should be considered the domain and which the codomain fur such function? Is the model pattern a function of the target pattern or vice versa? In all the cases studied from this point of view, there seems to have been no complication in assuming, without prior discussion, the model as the function that represents its target. But in section 2 it has been said that the model is not something that emerges as a function of the natural system it represents, but rather the natural system is what emerges from the world from its representation by a model. Therefore, this natural system is a function of the model and not vice versa.

A view that can be understood free of this warning is one that takes the models as set-theoretical structures. Even the account presented here could be thought of as very close to this view. In fact, the pattern that constitutes the formal nucleus of any model is a structure that can be thought of as isomorphic in relation to that of the target pattern, and the function that relates them is the identity function. Certainly, it would be an embedded isomorphism (...), i.e. embedded in the material model of the case. But is this the case actually? To consider it in such terms, there must be a function 𝑓:𝑋→𝑌 such that 𝑓(𝑥)=x where the domain X is embedded in the model and the codomain Y is embedded in the target. Here a series of adjustments must be made so that this function corresponds to the formal relationship between the respective patterns, since it has been admitted that the respective patterns do not coincide completely but only partially, and their coincidence increases as a possibility, this increase being the objective of the scientific normal activity itself.

There is a lot of juggling to put this in formal terms. It can be thought that there is a subset P of the codomain with elements that are only possible, not actually existing [checked?]. This could be thought of as a second function 𝑔:𝑋→𝑌 so that 𝑔(𝑥) is opaque in some sense for 𝑓(𝑥)∈P. That it is opaque could be formalized by taking that its elements have an associated possibility that goes from 0 to 1. Thus, it can be consider a binary operation 𝑔∘𝑓 such that 𝑔(𝑥) associates the elements of 𝑓(𝑥)∈P by a number that it goes from 0 to 1 according to a given distribution. This “association” is not a multiplication, since the actual value of each element (if it has a numerical value) does not change.

To this it must be added that for those elements actually existing in the target, e.i. for 𝑓(𝑥)∉P the identity prescribed by the function 𝑓(𝑥) is not always in general but approximate (pace the uncertainty in measurement). This could be formalized assuming that the function 𝑔(𝑥) is noisy (in some sense) for 𝑓(𝑥)∉P, this is, it multiplies each element of 𝑓(𝑥)∉P by a number close to 1 according to a given distribution.

All this can be considered correct only after assuming that some factors cannot be determined in general terms, but depend on the model in question each time, and even on the current state of the model in question:

  

- As explained in section 1.4 above, the range of subset P cannot be determined with any formula. [esta parece ser la única que queda con fuerza]
    
- The "opacity" of the elements of 𝑔(𝑥) cannot be determined a priori either. That is, the value between 0 and 1 by which each element of 𝑓(𝑥)∈P is by principle unknown in general terms. [se podría resolver con sucesivas contrastaciones, si es necesario]
    
- The "noise" of 𝑔(𝑥) also cannot be formally determined. The value for each element will tend to be closer to 1 according to an asymptotic curve for which a function cannot be established in principle. [esto no tiene que ver con la incertidumbre de la medición?]
    

  

Given these caveats, does it still make sense to bet on formalization to explain scientific models? Is there something lost if this notation stops being used? Does it even provide clarity in any sense? Can it help in any way to reach consensus as to what a scientific model is?

  

The question of whether this account can be defined as structuralist will remain as undefined as the question of whether it can do without structure.

  

In their great generality, structuralist accounts demand a "reconstruction" of current scientific theories with a structuralist key. In this account, the nuances given to the description of the structure lead to reconciliation with scientific practice as it has been occurring, and as a consequence it does not require, by method, any meta-theoretical condiment to knowledge as published in Journals, as presented at conferences and as exposed in class at all levels.


# Final comments


One reason the representation problem in ontological terms is not further analyzed here is that it refers to representation in general and not just to representation in science. Depending on how it is handled, it can even cover the vast semantic problem of oral and written language, according to which a word designates a thing in most general terms. Thus understood, it becomes an endless analysis, more considering the limits of the present work, which on the other hand does not need for its approach more than the clarification of the problem of representation in science in epistemic terms, and this is intended to have been adequately achieved. Every scientist is empowered to rest, in epistemic terms, on the formula "one is one", which means "every unitary element in the model claims to have its equivalent unitary element in the target". The formula is directly understandable and has functioned underground since science is science, despite the fact that the meaning of "is" in said formula is always indefinite.
  

Any two things can be thought of that have absolutely nothing to do with each other, and yet both coincide in that they are "things". Similarly, the unity of science does not consist in establishing the need to achieve a net that connects all the models that are presumed to be scientific so that the demarcation between what is science and what is not is reduced to a single though complex boundary. Rather, herein it is claimed to have shown that there is nothing to be achieved in this sense, not even to be understood, but rather to expose what has always been considered, that scientific models, however different they may be each other, they are scientific because they consist of conceptual systems, and thus the unity of science is reduced to the simplicity of scientific unity.

An enormous attempt has been made to show that to distinguish what is commonly called science it may be misguided to raise a model attached to science with formal or mathematical language, but rather to point out what in fact operates and has operated there whenever a knowledge system was called scientific: the conceptual unity. For this, the path has been forced to go to what is congenital to conceptual knowledge, which is representation. And the representation in turn guided the scientific element that concentrates its main components, the measuring instrument, in its very structure. The analysis of the measuring instrument led to the standard unit, which ended up becoming the foundation for the reliable establishment of any unitary system, with which it was intended to close the argumentative circle. Now the reader can judge whether the attempt has been neat and adequate enough to warrant further attention.
