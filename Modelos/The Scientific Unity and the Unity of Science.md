

# Final comments


One reason the representation problem in ontological terms is not further analyzed here is that it refers to representation in general and not just to representation in science. Depending on how it is handled, it can even cover the vast semantic problem of oral and written language, according to which a word designates a thing in most general terms. Thus understood, it becomes an endless analysis, more considering the limits of the present work, which on the other hand does not need for its approach more than the clarification of the problem of representation in science in epistemic terms, and this is intended to have been adequately achieved. Every scientist is empowered to rest, in epistemic terms, on the formula "one is one", which means "every unitary element in the model claims to have its equivalent unitary element in the target". The formula is directly understandable and has functioned underground since science is science, despite the fact that the meaning of "is" in said formula is always indefinite.
  

Any two things can be thought of that have absolutely nothing to do with each other, and yet both coincide in that they are "things". Similarly, the unity of science does not consist in establishing the need to achieve a net that connects all the models that are presumed to be scientific so that the demarcation between what is science and what is not is reduced to a single though complex boundary. Rather, herein it is claimed to have shown that there is nothing to be achieved in this sense, not even to be understood, but rather to expose what has always been considered, that scientific models, however different they may be each other, they are scientific because they consist of conceptual systems, and thus the unity of science is reduced to the simplicity of scientific unity.

An enormous attempt has been made to show that to distinguish what is commonly called science it may be misguided to raise a model attached to science with formal or mathematical language, but rather to point out what in fact operates and has operated there whenever a knowledge system was called scientific: the conceptual unity. For this, the path has been forced to go to what is congenital to conceptual knowledge, which is representation. And the representation in turn guided the scientific element that concentrates its main components, the measuring instrument, in its very structure. The analysis of the measuring instrument led to the standard unit, which ended up becoming the foundation for the reliable establishment of any unitary system, with which it was intended to close the argumentative circle. Now the reader can judge whether the attempt has been neat and adequate enough to warrant further attention.
