It is regrettable that Husserl’s reception within the philosophy of science has been largely mediated by cognitive and philosophy-of-mind frameworks that foreground intentionality, thereby obscuring the extent to which phenomenology was originally conceived as a foundational project for the sciences themselves rather than as a theory of mental states. *Eidetic variation*, as developed in _Ideas I_ (particularly §§2, 3, 4, 70–74), designates a methodological procedure intended to disclose the invariant structures that make an object what it is. Rather than taking essences as metaphysically given or as abstract universals, he situates them within an intentional practice: one varies the features of a given phenomenon—its shape, color, context, material, or other contingent determinations—and observes which aspects can be altered without the object ceasing to be the kind of thing it is. Those features that withstand systematic variation function as _eidetic invariants_. In this way the essence is not discovered through empirical generalization, but through a disciplined exploration of the space of possible configurations an object can assume while still fulfilling the same sense. Husserl emphasizes that this procedure is grounded in the phenomenological reduction, which brackets empirical assumptions in order to let the object appear in its pure givenness as a correlate of consciousness (Ideas I §§46–64).

This method places essences within a domain of active, rule-governed practice rather than metaphysical speculation. Eidetic insight arises from the performance of variations, not from any pre-existing conceptual taxonomy. Importantly, Husserl treats this as a _scientific_ method for the phenomenological sciences: the invariant structures thereby disclosed serve as the lawful forms that underwrite experience, cognition, and even the constitution of objects of science. The sciences, on his view, presuppose such eidetic structures even when they operate at a high level of abstraction, because scientific concepts ultimately depend on stable forms of sense that allow them to function as unities across different contexts of use. Consequently, eidetic variation is Husserl’s way of making the notion of “essence” compatible with a rigorous, practice-based understanding of scientific meaning.

Husserl develops phenomenology as a foundational science, drawing explicitly on the methodological achievements of the sciences of his time. Eidetic variation functions analogously to idealization procedures in physics and mathematics, where irrelevant parameters are systematically altered or suppressed in order to isolate lawful dependencies. The phenomenological reduction plays a role comparable to experimental control: by bracketing existential commitments, it stabilizes the field of investigation so that invariant relations can be discerned independently of contingent empirical noise. Likewise, his essences correspond less to metaphysical universals than to what his contemporary science would call invariant structures, symmetry conditions, or functional dependencies—features that remain constant across admissible transformations of a system.

In this respect, Husserl’s approach amounts to a highly original reconfiguration of ontology and metaphysics, making them methodologically dependent without dissolving them into method. Ontological claims about essences are no longer justified by speculative argument or metaphysical inheritance, but by their stability under regulated phenomenological procedures. What something _is_ can be articulated only through how it withstands eidetic variation within the phenomenological reduction. Yet this does not amount to a constructivist collapse of ontology into methodology: eidetic structures are not created by the method, but disclosed through it. Metaphysics is thus subordinated to method epistemically—its legitimacy depends on methodological access—while remaining ontologically irreducible.

Karen Barad’s agential realism provides conceptual resources for reconceiving Husserl’s eidetic method in non-transcendental, practice-oriented terms. In _Meeting the Universe Halfway_ (2007), Barad argues that phenomena are not pre-given objects but the results of _intra-actions_—mutual enactments of agencies of observation, material arrangements, and discursive practices. Concepts, on this view, do not represent independently existing essences; instead, they articulate patterns of determination produced within specific material-discursive configurations. An “object” becomes identifiable as this or that kind of entity only through _agential cuts_, which temporarily stabilize a boundary that differentiates subject from object, relevant from irrelevant, measurable from background. These cuts are not arbitrary but are enacted within established practices that coordinate instruments, norms, and embodied skills.

Seen from this perspective, Husserl’s eidetic variation can be reinterpreted as an enactment of differential constraints rather than a purely mental exploration of possibilities. The space of variations is not a private imaginative domain but a structured field shaped by communal practices, bodily dispositions, linguistic distinctions, and technically mediated ways of attending. What appears as an invariant in Husserl’s analysis—the “essence” that endures across variations—corresponds, in Barad’s terms, to a stability produced by the recurrence of certain agential cuts across different configurations. When one varies a phenomenon to see what can change without altering its identity, one is effectively probing the limits of the practices that sustain its intelligibility. The invariants that emerge are thus not timeless essences but the stabilized consequences of how material-discursive practices carve the world. In this way, Barad allows us to understand eidetic invariance not as a transcendental given but as a practice-dependent stabilization of sense.

From a Baradian standpoint, multimodal determinations in science, usually studied under the label of _robustness analysis_, can be understood as a materially enacted analogue of eidetic variation—one in which the “variations” are not imaginative but experimentally, instrumentally, and discursively realized. When scientists test a phenomenon through multiple independent methods, instruments, or modeling approaches, they are performing a kind of practical variation: they rearrange the _material-discursive_ configuration while tracking what features of the phenomenon remain stable. The invariants that survive these shifts correspond to what Husserl would have called the “essence,” but now reconceived as the outcome of repeated agential cuts that converge on a shared pattern. In this sense, _robustness_ is the empirical face of a Baradian, non-transcendental eidetic invariance: stability achieved through the recurrence of constraints across heterogeneous practices.

This perspective helps explain why scientific concepts acquire the apparent autonomy and unity with which scientists routinely treat them. In everyday scientific discourse, terms such as “electron,” “gene,” or “stress hormone” are used as if they picked out sharply bounded, context-independent units. Barad’s framework shows that this unity is not primordial but emergent from the convergence of multiple determination practices—measurements, experimental manipulations, representational conventions, computational models—that consistently enact the same agential cut. A concept appears isolated from context only because a dense network of material and discursive arrangements repeatedly stabilizes a particular pattern as a recognizable unit—much as Husserl’s eidetic variation stabilizes an invariant across imaginative transformations. Thus, the scientific sense of conceptual unity is not an illusion to be dispelled, nor a metaphysical given, but the practical achievement of a community whose iterative “variations” systematically reveal which features can vary and which must remain fixed for the phenomenon to count as _the same_.

In this framework, the degree to which a phenomenon appears as a _unitary object_ depends on the relational density of its context. When a phenomenon remains entangled within a diffuse or only partially articulated field of other phenomena, it presents itself with limited definition. But as successive agential cuts and investigative practices progressively _strip_ away peripheral relations—much as eidetic variation brackets accidental features while retaining invariant ones—a more stable pattern begins to emerge. This stripped pattern is not an isolated essence in the classical sense; rather, it is a recurrently enacted configuration whose robustness makes it functionally independent of many contextual fluctuations. Even so, no phenomenon becomes entirely free of background: every object still presupposes minimal relations that sustain its intelligibility, with reference-relations marking the limiting case in which one relation dominates sense-making. A phenomenon attains a scientific character when both it and the other phenomena to which it refers have been similarly _stripped_ through coordinated practices, allowing them to appear as units stable enough to sustain conceptual articulation. In this sense, the scientific concept arises when a phenomenon is stabilized in its unity by the mutually reinforcing operations of variation, cutting, and robust determination.

In sum, as the emergence of things is an effect of _intra-action_, what appears as a unitary object arises when a phenomenon is constituted through a relatively constrained network of relations, and when those relations themselves have already been stabilized as objects through prior practices. Where intra-actions proliferate without such stabilization, the phenomenon remains diffuse; where they are selectively narrowed to other robustly articulated objects, a determinate objecthood emerges. Scientific objectivity thus coincides with a regime of intra-action in which relational complexity is not eliminated but methodically bounded. Thus, scientific concepts emerge from processes in which phenomena are progressively _stripped_ of peripheral entanglements and stabilized through robust, multimodal determinations; yet even stripped objects retain relational embeddings that sustain their intelligibility.

To understand how reference—and eventually representation—arises from this relational field, it is necessary to articulate the basic principles governing how phenomena mutually determine one another within an enacted context. These principles outline how an object comes to be defined by its relations, how certain relations come to dominate, and how asymmetries in determination make reference possible without appealing to pre-given essences or mental intermediaries. The four principles presented next introduce to this dynamic in a highly stylized context with a small amount of objects just specified through the process explained above.

**Principle of reciprocity.**  Within any finite domain of phenomena, the definition of a given object—call it A—is jointly enacted through its intra-actions with the other elements of the domain (B through F). Barad's insight that relata emerge from relations allows us to say that A only becomes what it is through the patterned entanglements that also constitute B, C, D, and so on. Thus determination is reciprocal: A is constituted by its relations to B–F, and each of the other phenomena is likewise constituted in part through its relation to A. This does not imply symmetry but only that mutual participation is a structural condition for the existence of any phenomenon.

**Principle of irregularity.**  Although every phenomenon participates in the constitution of every other within a domain, not all relations contribute equally. Following Husserl’s idea that some variations modify inessential features while others strike at the core of the phenomenon, and Barad’s view that certain cuts foreground particular dimensions, we can say that the field of relations has uneven weightings. For A, some relations—say to C—play a more decisive role in stabilizing its identity than others, like its relation to E. This irregularity is not an imperfection; it is precisely what allows particular robust patterns to emerge from a complex relational background.

**Principle of polarization.**  When, among all the entanglements that help constitute A, a single relation contributes disproportionately to A’s stabilization, we can say that A _refers_ to B. Reference here is not a mental pointing or a pictorial match but the structural dominance of a particular relation in the enacted constitution of a stripped object. In robustness terms, B is the phenomenon whose multimodal traces most consistently anchor A’s identity under variation. Reference is thus the limiting case in which one relation exerts a markedly greater constitutive force than the others.

**Principle of asymmetry.**  Dominance in constitution does not entail reciprocity. Even if B plays the primary role in stabilizing A, A may contribute only marginally to the stabilization of B. This is a natural consequence of relational irregularity and of the directional character of many _agential cuts_. Asymmetry means that reference flows from A to B without requiring a corresponding determination from B to A, though such mutual dominance can occur contingently. This allows reference to be understood without presupposing bidirectional mapping or representational symmetry.

Consider as an example a painted animal on the wall of a cave and the living animal to which it refers—for instance, a zebu. Intuitively, one is inclined to say that there is _something_ in the painting that is also present in the animal, something in virtue of which the former can refer to the latter. Yet this formulation immediately generates a tension. The painted figure and the animal are clearly distinct phenomena, stabilized within different configurations of practice and interaction. To claim that one and the same thing is present in both seems to require either an abstract entity shared between them or a mysterious duplication of a single item across distinct situations—neither of which is admissible within the present framework.

The difficulty arises from an equivocation in the use of terms such as “the same” or “equal.” If these are understood as asserting numerical identity, the claim is plainly false: the painted figure and the animal are not one and the same thing. If they are understood as asserting equality in some formal or metric sense, the claim fares no better, since no such independent standard has yet been introduced. What the example instead forces into view is that reference does not require the sharing of a thing at all. The relation established between the painting and the animal is not mediated by an entity common to both, but by a convergence in how each is organized and stabilized relative to certain possible variations.

What connects the two cases is therefore not an object, property, or component that could be isolated and compared across them, but a mode of organization that governs how changes matter for their identification. Within the relevant practices, both the painted figure and the animal respond in aligned ways to systematic variation: some alterations leave what is depicted or encountered recognizable as the same kind of animal, while others do not. The reference is grounded in this alignment of sensitivities—an identity of role under regulated variation—rather than in the presence of an identical constituent. The recurrence involved here is practical and relational, not ontological. 

The four principles aim to clarify how reference can emerge from within a Baradian, practice-based ontology without importing metaphysical dualisms or mental intermediaries. A scientific concept can refer because, through stripping, variation, and cut-based stabilization, one phenomenon becomes the dominant anchor for another within a structured field of relations. On this view, scientific representation, which can be considered a specific and more constrained form of reference, is not a mirroring but a relational asymmetry sustained by robust practices. If reference designates the broad field of relational dependencies through which phenomena acquire definition, scientific representation names a narrower configuration within that field—one in which the alignment between phenomena is explicitly cultivated and stabilized. A scientific representation requires that both the model and the target be the result of independent processes of stabilization, each shaped by multimodal determinations that selectively bound background relations until a relatively invariant organization is achieved. Only once such organizational stability, herein called pattern, is in place does it become meaningful to speak of a representational alignment between them. So robust practices acts here as a string condition for the reference to be a scientific representation.

Here the model should not be understood as an abstract structure—indeed, the very opposition between abstract and concrete risks obscuring what is at stake—but as a device in which a stabilized organization (the pattern) is embedded and made operable. An equation set, a diagram, a simulation, or an experimental setup functions as a model insofar as it enacts, within a specific practice, a robust configuration of relations oriented toward reference. Different devices may therefore count as distinct models while still operating as models of the same phenomenon, provided that they are stabilized so as to support the same role under regulated variation and can be aligned with the same target on that basis.

Since robustness enables representation, it does not by itself generate it. The representational relation emerges only when practices enact a mapping that preserves a relevant aspect of the organization stabilized in the model and independently manifested in the target. Model and target must therefore not merely exhibit convergent organizations; they must be co-articulated through enacted alignments that render those organizations mutually intelligible within a practice. This co-articulation, however, is not symmetrical. The model, as a device stabilized to operate _about_ something else, is selectively cut and oriented so as to function representationally with respect to a particular target system. The target, by contrast, is stabilized as an object of investigation and at most technologically arranged to manifest its own organization, not to enter into a privileged relation with the model itself. Scientific representation thus sits at the intersection of two independently stabilized phenomena and a practice-forged linkage between them—one that assigns representational function to the model while leaving the target epistemically anchored in its own mode of stabilization.

The notion of pattern at work here should not be understood as denoting an additional entity shared by model and target, nor as a formal structure instantiated in two distinct systems, as in standard structuralist or isomorphism-based accounts of representation. What is preserved across model and target is not a mapping between elements that exhaustively mirrors their relations, but a stability of organization under a controlled space of admissible variations. A pattern does not exist independently of its concrete embeddings, nor can it be specified as an abstract relational schema prior to the practices that stabilize it. Instead, it designates a coherence of roles: a way in which certain relations are treated as constitutive and others as negligible for the purposes at hand. Two systems therefore count as exhibiting the same pattern not because there exists a bijective correspondence between their components, but because the same transformations are regarded as irrelevant and the same disruptions as significant within a shared regime of determination. This sharply distinguishes the present account from structural realist views that posit a single structure underlying both model and world, as well as from semantic views that appeal to partial isomorphism as the ground of representation. Here, the identity of the pattern is neither guaranteed by formal equivalence nor by ontological commitment, but by the robustness of an ordering enacted and sustained within practice. Material heterogeneity thus poses no obstacle, since the pattern is identified not by the intrinsic properties of its elements but by the constraints governing their relational organization. In this sense, the pattern is not itself symbolic or empirical; it becomes articulable under either category only insofar as the stabilized ordering is mobilized for representational alignment or empirical determination.

At this point it becomes legitimate to introduce a distinction between an _empirical_ and a _symbolic_ ambit within scientific practice, not as an ontological dichotomy but as a functional differentiation grounded in reference and representation. The empirical ambit comprises phenomena insofar as they are stabilized as objects of investigation through robust determination, while the symbolic ambit comprises those stabilized configurations that are specifically oriented to operate _about_ other phenomena. What justifies this distinction is not a difference in kind between entities, but a difference in role within a relational economy: symbolic devices are constituted so that one of their relations—reference to a target—dominates their function, whereas target systems are constituted without any corresponding orientation toward the model. The symbolic ambit is thus the space in which asymmetries of reference are institutionally sustained and made productive for inference, articulation, and coordination, while the empirical ambit anchors those symbolic operations in phenomena whose stabilization does not depend on representational function.

This symbolic–empirical differentiation, though derived primarily from Husserlian and Baradian frameworks, extends naturally to several contemporary accounts of scientific modeling. Mari explicitly characterizes models as symbolic systems whose epistemic role consists in standing in a rule-governed relation to empirical systems (“models are symbolic artifacts used to gain knowledge about empirical systems,” Mari 2005, p. 7), while stressing that this does not entail an abstract–concrete dualism. Van Fraassen similarly treats models as abstract structures employed to represent phenomena, emphasizing that representation is “something scientists do” rather than a relation grounded in resemblance or mirroring (van Fraassen 1980, pp. 42–44; 2008, p. 23). Giere’s agent-based account reinforces this asymmetry by locating representation in the purposive use of models by agents: “a model represents a real system only for some purpose, by some user” (Giere 1988, p. 80). Across these approaches, what recurs is a commitment to an account of representation that presupposes neither ontological dualism nor semantic reduction, but instead a structured asymmetry in how stabilized configurations are mobilized within scientific practice.

The symbolic–empirical differentiation can, in this context, be understood as a category distinction in Ryle’s sense: not a division between entities of different kinds, but a differentiation between modes of description grounded in distinct roles within scientific practice. The same stabilized pattern may be apprehended under the symbolic category when it is embedded in a device oriented toward representation, and under the empirical category when it is stabilized as a phenomenon to be investigated. What differs is not the structure itself, but the category under which it is taken up—one emphasizing its role in inferential articulation and reference, the other its role as an object of determination. This way of speaking preserves the possibility that model and target share a common structure, while denying that they therefore occupy the same logical role. Several contemporary accounts implicitly rely on such category distinctions without naming them as such.

Giere’s agent-relative theory of representation presupposes that the same structure can function either as a model or as a system modeled, depending on how it is situated within a representational practice (Giere 1988; 2004). Van Fraassen likewise distinguishes between structures _as used_ in representation and structures _as exhibited_ by phenomena, while explicitly resisting any attempt to ground this distinction in ontology rather than in scientific practice (van Fraassen 2008, ch. 5). Mari’s insistence that models are symbolic systems related to empirical systems through rule-governed mappings similarly presupposes a categorical difference of role rather than of constitution (Mari 2005). Other approaches—particularly those that seek to treat representational structure as exhaustively characterizing both models and their targets—risk blurring this differentiation by leaving the functional asymmetry of representation underarticulated. The present framework makes explicit what a range of practice-sensitive accounts presuppose in different ways: that symbolic and empirical ambits mark distinct categories of articulation within scientific inquiry, under which the same patterned organization may legitimately appear without ontological duplication.
